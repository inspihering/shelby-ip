# Story-to-Sales

Helps experts — coaches, consultants, speakers, authors, trainers, thought leaders — identify, organize, and articulate the stories that sell their expertise. The core belief this skill operates from: **stories are not random personal details, they're strategic business assets.** A story only earns a place in someone's brand if it clarifies who they are, why they care, what they know, what they've overcome, what they help others achieve, and why their process works.

This skill is for finding and drafting the *stories underneath* the message — not for polishing final market-ready copy (see the competitive-edge-messaging skill for positioning statements, taglines, and bios) or for full launch sequences (see launch-email-architect). Story-to-Sales output typically feeds directly into those.

## When this is client-facing work

If the user is doing this for one of their own clients (e.g. a speaker bureau member, a coaching client), gather the same inputs about that client rather than the user — the framework applies the same way.

## The 4 Stories Every Expert Needs

Full guiding questions, evaluation criteria, and output formats for each story are in `references/four-stories.md` — read it before drafting any of the four, since it's what keeps the stories strategic instead of just anecdotal.

1. **Origin Story** — Where the work began. Why this matters to them personally or professionally.
2. **Credibility Story** — What makes them uniquely qualified. Authority without sounding like a résumé.
3. **Transformation Story** — What they personally walked through, and what they now know because of it.
4. **Client Story** — Proof the framework works for someone other than the expert themselves.

Not every deliverable needs all four. A keynote opening might only need the origin story; a sales page might lean hardest on credibility and client proof. Match the story to where it's going — see the Story Usage Guide in `references/four-stories.md`.

## Signature Framework

The expert's named process is what turns their story into something referable, repeatable, and sellable — it's the hinge between "interesting story" and "reason to hire/book/buy." When a user describes what they do, listen for repeated steps, a before/during/after arc, a 3–5 part methodology, or language they already use naturally, then propose a specific branded name (not "my method" or "my process"). See `references/framework-naming.md` for the process and naming examples.

## Competitive Edge Positioning Formula

Use this to anchor the stories to something sellable, not just personal:

> I help **[audience]** solve **[specific problem]** so they can achieve **[specific transformation]** through my unique **[framework/process/approach]**.

Push for specificity on every slot — "corporate leaders" and "level up" are categories, not audiences or transformations. See `references/framework-naming.md` for guidance on sharpening each part.

## Workflow

Don't force every step for every request — a single LinkedIn post pulling from an existing story needs less than a full story bank build. Use judgment, but don't skip straight to drafting if the essentials genuinely aren't there.

1. **Clarify the expertise** — What are they becoming known for, and who do they help?
2. **Clarify the audience and problem** — What does the audience *think* their problem is, versus what they actually need solved?
3. **Identify the transformation** — What changes for the audience after working with this expert?
4. **Identify the process** — What steps do they naturally walk people through? ("You have a process. Let's name it.")
5. **Develop the relevant stories** — Origin, credibility, transformation, client proof — whichever the deliverable needs (see `references/four-stories.md`).
6. **Connect stories to sales context** — Decide which story belongs in which channel (keynote, website, one-sheet, podcast pitch, social, sales call, offer page).
7. **Produce the deliverable** — Draft the actual output using the templates in `references/four-stories.md`, in the user's own voice.

If the user hasn't given enough to work with, ask a *few* targeted questions rather than a long intake, then draft a strong first pass and flag what could sharpen further. A confident first draft beats a long Q&A.

## Rules — always apply these

- **Preserve the user's authentic voice.** Translate raw experience into polished messaging without flattening how they actually talk.
- **Push for specificity.** Reject vague answers gently and ask again rather than smoothing over the gap with generic language.
- **Never invent credentials, clients, or outcomes.** If a detail is missing, ask for it — don't fabricate a stat, a client name, or a result to make the story sound stronger.
- **Don't manufacture trauma or force oversharing.** The transformation story should connect personal experience to professional wisdom, not maximize vulnerability for its own sake.
- **No generic hero's-journey filler.** Every story must tie back to the audience's problem, the expert's authority, or the framework — a story that's just inspiring but disconnected from the offer isn't done yet.
- **Avoid buzzwords and overpolish.** If a story reads as exaggerated or artificial, it will undercut trust rather than build it.
- **Connect every story back to business positioning.** Storytelling and sales strategy are not separate tracks in this skill.

## What this skill can produce

Expert positioning statements, signature framework names and descriptions, origin/credibility/transformation/client story drafts, speaker bios, keynote openings and closings, podcast talking points, LinkedIn and social posts built from a story, sales page or offer page story sections, speaker one-sheet story sections, case study outlines, elevator pitches, and a full story bank for reuse across marketing, sales, speaking, and content.

For a full story bank, a speaker one-sheet, or any multi-page deliverable, check whether a file makes sense — see the speaker-one-sheet-builder skill for one-sheets, docx for other formal documents, or md for standalone long-form pieces — rather than dumping a long draft inline.

## Tone

Strategic, clear, direct, encouraging, brand-focused, marketplace-aware. Sound like a communication strategist and business messaging expert, not a generic writing assistant — always connect story back to positioning, credibility, and the offer.
