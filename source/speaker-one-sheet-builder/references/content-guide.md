# Content Guide

Reasoning and examples for each section of a speaker one sheet. Use this to draft copy, not as a form to fill in mechanically — the goal is a document that makes a specific speaker feel inevitable to book, not a document that could describe anyone in their category.

## 1. Speaker positioning

Before writing anything else, get the positioning statement right — everything else on the one sheet either supports or contradicts it.

Ask (directly, or infer from what the user has already told you about their work):
- Who are you known for helping, and with what specific problem?
- What changes for the audience because of you?
- What's the one thing you want an event planner to remember?

**Positioning statement formula:**
"[Name] helps [specific audience] solve [specific problem] so they can achieve [specific transformation] through [their unique framework or perspective]."

**Brand statement example** (longer, more narrative):
"Shelby Jo Long helps organizations turn disagreement into a competitive advantage by teaching leaders how to use communication, conflict, and constructive dissent to make better decisions and build stronger cultures."

The test for a good positioning statement: could a competitor in the same space claim the exact same sentence? If yes, it's not specific enough yet.

## 2. Best-fit audiences

Don't try to appeal to everyone — a one sheet that lists ten industries reads as unfocused, not versatile. Name the 4-7 buyer types and room types where this speaker is genuinely the strongest fit.

Two angles to define:
- **Who books them** (leadership conferences, associations, HR/talent teams, sales orgs, healthcare organizations, women's leadership events, executive retreats, chambers of commerce, etc.)
- **Who's in the room** (executives, founders, managers, emerging leaders, sales teams, healthcare leaders, professional associations, etc.)

Output as a short scannable list, e.g.:
> This keynote is ideal for: leadership conferences · executive retreats · corporate training events · association meetings · women's leadership summits · culture and communication events

## 3. Signature keynote / speaking offers

Every one sheet needs one anchor offer, even if the speaker has several topics. If the user doesn't have a clear "signature," help them pick the topic most aligned with their positioning and build that out as the anchor; other topics go in a supporting list.

**Format:**
- **Title:** a compelling, specific keynote title (not "Leadership Excellence" — something with a point of view)
- **Subtitle:** one promise-driven line
- **Description:** 100-150 words, written for an event planner skimming a dozen of these — lead with the problem the audience doesn't realize they have, then what the speaker delivers
- **Audience takeaways:** 3-4 bullets starting with action verbs (Understand / Apply / Shift / Strengthen), each tied to something concrete, not just a feeling

## 4. Additional speaking topics (if needed)

3-5 topics, each with: title, 2-3 sentence description, best-fit audience, and 2-3 takeaways. Keep these tight — this section supports the signature keynote, it shouldn't compete with it for attention.

Example structure:
> **Agree to Disagree — How Great Companies Turn Conflict into a Competitive Advantage**
> Most organizations avoid disagreement because they assume silence means harmony. In reality, silence often signals disengagement or missed innovation. This session teaches leaders how to use productive disagreement as a tool for stronger decisions and higher-performing teams.
> Best for: leadership conferences, executive retreats, HR events, culture-building programs.

## 5. Biography

Draft the length(s) the layout calls for; a short bio (60-100 words) is standard for the one sheet itself. If the user wants the full set for other uses (40-word, 75-word, 150-word, full intro, emcee intro, media bio), that's a quick follow-up once the one sheet copy is locked, not something to generate speculatively up front.

**Short bio formula:**
"[Name] is a [title/role] who helps [audience] achieve [result]. With expertise in [credential areas], [Name] brings [distinct perspective] to help audiences [transformation]."

A good bio has: professional identity, the signature message, one or two credibility anchors, and the audience transformation — in that order of priority. Personal story only earns space if it's the actual source of the speaker's authority on the topic (not decoration).

## 6. Credibility snapshot

5-7 lines, not a CV. Pull from: professional/founder titles, books authored, media appearances, certifications, notable stages, proprietary frameworks, client results, years/organizations served, awards.

Prioritize lines that connect directly to the speaking topic over generically impressive ones. "Creator of the CDE (Create. Debate. Evaluate.) framework" earns its place on a conflict-and-decision-making one sheet; a marathon finish time doesn't, even if impressive.

## 7. Organizations served

Use whichever the user has real material for — don't fabricate names or logos:
- **Logos:** "As Seen At / Trusted By" with actual permitted logos
- **Named list:** if the speaker has permission to name specific organizations
- **Industry framing** (safest default when specific names aren't confirmed): "Experience serving healthcare organizations, leadership associations, entrepreneurship communities, and corporate teams"

Never invent organization names or claim logos without the user confirming they have permission to use them.

## 8. Testimonials

Ask what event planners, audience members, or clients have actually said — then tighten to the single most powerful sentence rather than including the whole quote. A testimonial that names a specific result ("increased close rates," "changed how our leadership team runs meetings") outperforms generic praise ("amazing speaker!").

If the user has no testimonials yet, leave the section out rather than inventing one — an absent testimonials section is normal for a newer speaker; a fabricated one is a credibility risk.

## 9. Audience transformation

One tight paragraph: what does the audience struggle with before, what shifts during the talk, what can they do differently after. This usually lives near the top of the one sheet (under the positioning statement) or folded into the signature keynote description — it's the emotional core of the pitch, so don't bury it at the bottom.

Example: "Participants leave with a new way to approach disagreement, practical tools for productive conversations, and the confidence to use communication as a leadership advantage."

## 10. Booking section

Whatever the user has: website, email, phone, speaker reel link, social handles. Always include a direct call to action ("Book [Name] for your next event" / "Available for keynotes, workshops, and executive retreats").
